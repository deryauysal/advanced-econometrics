2  Method of Moments and GMM

\[ \newcommand{\bm}[1]{\boldsymbol{#1}} \newcommand{\btheta}{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \newcommand{\bbeta}{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \newcommand{\bgamma}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}} \newcommand{\bx}{\boldsymbol{x}} \newcommand{\by}{\boldsymbol{y}} \newcommand{\bX}{\boldsymbol{X}} \newcommand{\bR}{\boldsymbol{R}} \newcommand{\br}{\boldsymbol{r}} \newcommand{\bh}{\boldsymbol{h}} \newcommand{\bz}{\boldsymbol{z}} \newcommand{\bZ}{\boldsymbol{Z}} \newcommand{\bW}{\boldsymbol{W}} \newcommand{\bA}{\boldsymbol{A}} \newcommand{\bV}{\boldsymbol{V}} \newcommand{\bu}{\boldsymbol{u}} \newcommand{\bw}{\boldsymbol{w}} \newcommand{\bpsi}{\boldsymbol{\psi}} \newcommand{\balpha}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \newcommand{\bdelta}{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \newcommand{\bmu}{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \newcommand{\bg}{\boldsymbol{g}} \newcommand{\bS}{\boldsymbol{S}} \newcommand{\bD}{\boldsymbol{D}} \newcommand{\bI}{\boldsymbol{I}} \newcommand{\E}{\mathbb{E}} \newcommand{\Var}{\operatorname{Var}} \newcommand{\AVar}{\operatorname{Avar}} \newcommand{\cvp}{\xrightarrow{\;p\;}} \newcommand{\cvd}{\xrightarrow{\;d\;}} \newcommand{\iid}{\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}} \newcommand{\KLIC}{\operatorname{KLIC}} \newcommand{\pd}[2]{\frac{\partial #1}{\partial #2}} \]

Readings

Cameron and Trivedi (2005), Ch. 6.1–6.5; B. E. Hansen (2022), Ch. 12 (instrumental variables) and Ch. 13 (GMM); Wooldridge (2010), Ch. 8 and 14. Applications: Arellano and Bond (1991) and Imai and Ratkovic (2014).

Maximum likelihood requires the full distribution of the data. Many economic models deliver less: a set of expectations that should be zero at the true parameter value. The method of moments (MM) and its generalization, the generalized method of moments (GMM), turn such moment conditions directly into estimators. OLS, IV, 2SLS and ML are all special cases, and the GMM framework gives a single recipe for their asymptotic distribution and for efficient estimation when there are more conditions than parameters (L. P. Hansen 1982).

2.1 Moment conditions

Let \(\bw_i\) collect all observed variables for unit \(i\) (outcomes, regressors, instruments) and let \(\btheta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^k\).

Definition 2.1 (Moment function) A \(q\times1\) vector function \(\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta)\) with \(q\ge k\) is a moment function if \[ \E\big[\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)\big]=\bm 0 . \] This equation is a (population) moment condition or moment restriction.

A moment function depends only on the data and the parameters, it has at least as many elements as there are parameters, and its expectation is zero at the true value. Three examples recur throughout the chapter.

Example 2.1 (Familiar estimators as moment estimators)  

  1. Mean. \(\psi(y_i,\mu)=y_i-\mu\). The sample analogue \(\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu)=0\) gives \(\hat\mu=\bar y\).
  2. OLS. In \(y_i=\bx_i'\bbeta+u_i\), the assumption \(\E[u_i\mid\bx_i]=0\) implies the \(k\) conditions \(\E[\bx_i(y_i-\bx_i'\bbeta)]=\bm 0\). Solving the sample analogue gives \(\hat\bbeta=\big(\sum_i\bx_i\bx_i'\big)^{-1}\sum_i\bx_iy_i\).
  3. Maximum likelihood. Under correct specification the score has mean zero (Theorem 1.2). With \(\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta)=\partial\ln f(y_i\mid\bx_i;\btheta)/\partial\btheta\), the MM estimator is the root of the score, i.e. the MLE.

2.2 The method of moments estimator

Definition 2.2 (Method of moments estimator) If \(q=k\), the MM estimator \(\hat\btheta\) solves the \(k\) sample moment conditions \[ \bar\bg_n(\hat\btheta)\equiv\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\bpsi(\bw_i,\hat\btheta)=\bm 0 . \]

MM estimators are consistent whenever a law of large numbers makes the sample moments converge to the population moments and \(\btheta_0\) is the unique solution of \(\E[\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta)]=\bm 0\). For OLS, \[ \hat\bbeta=\bbeta+\Big(\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\bx_i\bx_i'\Big)^{-1}\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\bx_iu_i\ \cvp\ \bbeta+\E[\bx_i\bx_i']^{-1}\E[\bx_iu_i]=\bbeta, \] provided \(\E[\bx_i\bx_i']\) is nonsingular.

Theorem 2.1 (Asymptotic distribution of the MM estimator) Under regularity conditions and i.i.d. sampling, \[ \sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\cvd N\big(\bm 0,\ \bA_0^{-1}\bV_0\bA_0^{-1\prime}\big),\qquad \bA_0=\E\Big[\pd{\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)}{\btheta'}\Big],\quad \bV_0=\E\big[\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)'\big]. \]

Sketch. A mean-value expansion of \(\bar\bg_n(\hat\btheta)=\bm 0\) around \(\btheta_0\) gives \(\bm 0=\bar\bg_n(\btheta_0)+\bA_n(\bar\btheta)(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\) with \(\bA_n=\frac1n\sum_i\partial\bpsi_i/\partial\btheta'\). Hence \(\sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)=-\bA_n^{-1}\sqrt n\,\bar\bg_n(\btheta_0)\). The LLN gives \(\bA_n\cvp\bA_0\), the CLT gives \(\sqrt n\,\bar\bg_n(\btheta_0)\cvd N(\bm 0,\bV_0)\), and Slutsky does the rest. This is the same argument as for the MLE in Chapter 1; the MLE simply has \(\bA_0=-\mathcal I_1\) and \(\bV_0=\mathcal I_1\).

The variance is estimated by the sandwich \(\hat\bA^{-1}\hat\bV\hat\bA^{-1\prime}\) with \(\hat\bA=\frac1n\sum_i\partial\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta)/\partial\btheta'\) and \(\hat\bV=\frac1n\sum_i\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta)\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta)'\).

Example 2.2 (OLS under heteroskedasticity) With \(\bpsi=\bx_i(y_i-\bx_i'\bbeta)\) we get \(\bA_0=-\E[\bx_i\bx_i']\) and \(\bV_0=\E[u_i^2\bx_i\bx_i']\), so \[ \sqrt n(\hat\bbeta-\bbeta)\cvd N\big(\bm 0,\ \E[\bx_i\bx_i']^{-1}\E[u_i^2\bx_i\bx_i']\E[\bx_i\bx_i']^{-1}\big). \] White’s heteroskedasticity-robust variance estimator is the MM sandwich for OLS. Likewise, the PML sandwich in Section 1.6 is the MM sandwich with the score as moment function.

2.3 The generalized method of moments

When \(q>k\) the model is overidentified: in general no \(\btheta\) sets all \(q\) sample moments to zero at once. GMM instead makes them as small as possible in a quadratic metric.

Example 2.3 (Two sets of conditions for one regression) If, in addition to \(\E[u_i\mid\bx_i]=0\), the errors are conditionally symmetric, then \(\E[u_i^3\mid\bx_i]=0\) and \[ \E\begin{pmatrix}\bx_i(y_i-\bx_i'\bbeta)\\ \bx_i(y_i-\bx_i'\bbeta)^3\end{pmatrix}=\bm 0 , \] which gives \(2k\) conditions for \(k\) parameters.

Definition 2.3 (GMM estimator) Let \(\bW_n\) be a \(q\times q\) symmetric positive definite weighting matrix, possibly data dependent. The GMM estimator is \[ \hat\btheta(\bW_n)=\arg\min_{\btheta\in\Theta}\ Q_n(\btheta),\qquad Q_n(\btheta)=\bar\bg_n(\btheta)'\,\bW_n\,\bar\bg_n(\btheta). \]

The first-order conditions are \[ \bA_n(\hat\btheta)'\,\bW_n\,\bar\bg_n(\hat\btheta)=\bm 0,\qquad \bA_n(\btheta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\pd{\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta)}{\btheta'} , \] i.e. GMM sets \(k\) linear combinations of the \(q\) sample moments to zero. If \(q=k\), \(\bA_n\) is square and (generically) invertible, so the FOC reduce to \(\bar\bg_n(\hat\btheta)=\bm 0\): in the just-identified case GMM is MM, whatever \(\bW_n\).

2.4 Asymptotic properties of GMM

Theorem 2.2 (Consistency and asymptotic normality of GMM) Assume i.i.d. data and

  1. identification: \(\E[\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta)]=\bm 0\) if and only if \(\btheta=\btheta_0\);

  2. \(\bA_0=\E[\partial\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)/\partial\btheta']\) is finite with full column rank \(k\);

  3. \(\bW_n\cvp\bW_0\), symmetric positive definite;

  4. \(\sqrt n\,\bar\bg_n(\btheta_0)\cvd N(\bm 0,\bV_0)\), \(\bV_0=\E[\bpsi_i\bpsi_i']\) positive definite;

plus standard regularity conditions (compact \(\Theta\), uniform convergence, smoothness). Then \(\hat\btheta\cvp\btheta_0\) and \[ \sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\cvd N\Big(\bm 0,\ (\bA_0'\bW_0\bA_0)^{-1}\bA_0'\bW_0\bV_0\bW_0\bA_0(\bA_0'\bW_0\bA_0)^{-1}\Big). \]

The proof follows the MM sketch: expand the FOC around \(\btheta_0\) and solve, \(\sqrt n(\hat\btheta-\btheta_0)\approx-(\bA_0'\bW_0\bA_0)^{-1}\bA_0'\bW_0\sqrt n\,\bar\bg_n(\btheta_0)\). Two features are worth stressing. First, the weighting matrix does not affect consistency, only the asymptotic variance. Second, condition (ii) is the local version of identification: if \(\bA_0\) has deficient rank, the moments do not pin down all parameters, which is the essence of weak or irrelevant instruments.

The variance is estimated by replacing \(\bA_0,\bV_0,\bW_0\) by \(\hat\bA=\bA_n(\hat\btheta)\), \(\hat\bV=\frac1n\sum_i\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta)\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta)'\) and \(\bW_n\), and dividing by \(n\).

2.5 Efficient GMM

Theorem 2.3 (Optimal weighting matrix) Among all choices of \(\bW_0\), \(\bW_0^*=\bV_0^{-1}\) minimizes the asymptotic variance (in the matrix sense of Chapter 1). The minimum is \[ \AVar\big(\hat\btheta(\bV_0^{-1})\big)=\big(\bA_0'\bV_0^{-1}\bA_0\big)^{-1}. \]

Proof. Write \(\bm B=(\bA'\bV^{-1}\bA)^{-1}\bA'\bV^{-1}\) and \(\bm C=(\bA'\bW\bA)^{-1}\bA'\bW-\bm B\) (dropping subscripts). The asymptotic variance with weight \(\bW\) is \((\bm B+\bm C)\bV(\bm B+\bm C)'\). The cross term vanishes because \(\bm C\bV\bm B'=\big[(\bA'\bW\bA)^{-1}\bA'\bW\bA-(\bA'\bV^{-1}\bA)^{-1}\bA'\bV^{-1}\bA\big](\bA'\bV^{-1}\bA)^{-1}=\bm 0\). Hence the variance equals \((\bA'\bV^{-1}\bA)^{-1}+\bm C\bV\bm C'\), and \(\bm C\bV\bm C'\) is positive semidefinite. \(\square\)

Intuitively, moments that are measured with more noise get less weight. “Optimal” is relative to a given set of moment conditions: poorly chosen moments remain inefficient even with the best weight. Adding valid moment conditions never increases the asymptotic variance of efficient GMM.

\(\bV_0\) is unknown, so efficient GMM is implemented in steps:

  1. Estimate \(\hat\btheta^{(1)}\) with a convenient weight, e.g. \(\bW_n=\bI\) or, in linear IV, \((\bZ'\bZ/n)^{-1}\). It is consistent but inefficient.
  2. Compute \(\hat\bV=\frac1n\sum_i\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta^{(1)})\bpsi_i(\hat\btheta^{(1)})'\) and re-estimate with \(\bW_n=\hat\bV^{-1}\). This two-step GMM estimator is asymptotically efficient.

Repeating step 2 until convergence gives iterated GMM; letting the weight depend on \(\btheta\) inside the objective, \(Q_n(\btheta)=\bar\bg_n(\btheta)'\hat\bV(\btheta)^{-1}\bar\bg_n(\btheta)\), gives the continuously updated estimator (CUE). All three are asymptotically equivalent. In finite samples, estimating \(\bV_0\) introduces bias, especially with many moment conditions (Altonji and Segal 1996), so efficient GMM need not beat a simple one-step estimator in practice.

2.6 Testing overidentifying restrictions

GMM consistency rests on \(\E[\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)]=\bm 0\). With \(q>k\) there is information left to check it: at the efficient estimate the sample moments should be close to zero.

Theorem 2.4 (Hansen’s J test (L. P. Hansen 1982)) Let \(\hat\btheta\) be the efficient GMM estimator and \(\hat\bV\) a consistent estimator of \(\bV_0\). Under \(H_0:\E[\bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta_0)]=\bm 0\), \[ J=n\,\bar\bg_n(\hat\btheta)'\,\hat\bV^{-1}\,\bar\bg_n(\hat\btheta)\ \cvd\ \chi^2_{q-k}. \]

Three remarks. The factor \(n\) matters: without it the statistic converges to zero. The degrees of freedom are the number of overidentifying restrictions, \(q-k\); in a just-identified model \(J=0\) by construction and nothing can be tested. Finally, the test has power only against violations that cannot be absorbed by \(\btheta\): if all instruments are invalid in the same direction, \(J\) may not reject. A rejection says that some moment conditions fail, not which.

2.7 Conditional moments and optimal instruments

Models are often stated as a conditional restriction \(\E[\rho(\bw_i,\btheta_0)\mid\bz_i]=0\), e.g. \(\rho=y_i-\bx_i'\bbeta\) with exogenous \(\bz_i\). By the law of iterated expectations, any function \(\bm D(\bz_i)\) yields valid unconditional moments \(\E[\bm D(\bz_i)\rho(\bw_i,\btheta_0)]=\bm 0\), so there are infinitely many GMM estimators. The asymptotically best choice, the optimal instrument, is \[ \bm D^*(\bz_i)=\E\Big[\pd{\rho(\bw_i,\btheta_0)}{\btheta}\,\Big|\,\bz_i\Big]\Big/\Var\big(\rho(\bw_i,\btheta_0)\mid\bz_i\big). \] In the linear model with exogenous regressors (\(\bz_i=\bx_i\)) this is \(-\bx_i/\sigma_i^2\): the efficient moment estimator is weighted least squares (GLS). With endogenous regressors it is \(-\E[\bx_i\mid\bz_i]/\sigma_i^2\), which motivates 2SLS: the first stage approximates \(\E[\bx_i\mid\bz_i]\) by a linear projection.

2.8 Linear instrumental variables as GMM

Consider \(y_i=\bx_i'\bbeta+u_i\) with \(\bx_i\) of dimension \(k\) and some regressors correlated with \(u_i\) (omitted variables, measurement error, simultaneity). OLS is inconsistent. Let \(\bz_i\) be an \(l\times1\) vector of instruments, containing the exogenous regressors, with

  • exogeneity: \(\E[\bz_iu_i]=\bm 0\);
  • relevance: \(\operatorname{rank}\E[\bz_i\bx_i']=k\) (requires \(l\ge k\)), and \(\E[\bz_i\bz_i']\) nonsingular.

The moment function is \(\bpsi(\bw_i,\bbeta)=\bz_i(y_i-\bx_i'\bbeta)\), with sample analogue \(\frac1n\bZ'(\by-\bX\bbeta)\) in matrix notation. Minimizing \(\frac1{n^2}(\by-\bX\bbeta)'\bZ\bW_n\bZ'(\by-\bX\bbeta)\) gives the closed form \[ \hat\bbeta(\bW_n)=\big(\bX'\bZ\bW_n\bZ'\bX\big)^{-1}\bX'\bZ\bW_n\bZ'\by . \] Here \(\bA_0=-\E[\bz_i\bx_i']\) and \(\bV_0=\E[u_i^2\bz_i\bz_i']\). The familiar estimators are special cases.

2SLS is the GMM estimator with \(\bW_n=(\bZ'\bZ)^{-1}\); under homoskedasticity, \(\bV_0=\sigma^2\E[\bz_i\bz_i']\) and this weight is optimal. Under heteroskedasticity both 2SLS and two-step GMM are consistent, GMM is more efficient, and 2SLS standard errors must be heteroskedasticity robust: \[ \widehat{\Var}(\hat\bbeta)=\big(\bX'\bZ\bW_n\bZ'\bX\big)^{-1}\bX'\bZ\bW_n\Big(\sum_i\hat u_i^2\bz_i\bz_i'\Big)\bW_n\bZ'\bX\big(\bX'\bZ\bW_n\bZ'\bX\big)^{-1}. \] For two-step GMM this simplifies to \(\big[\bX'\bZ\big(\sum_i\hat u_i^2\bz_i\bz_i'\big)^{-1}\bZ'\bX\big]^{-1}\).

Weak instruments. If the instruments are only weakly correlated with the endogenous regressors, \(\bA_0\) is close to rank deficient. IV and 2SLS are then biased towards OLS and their normal approximation is poor, even in large samples (Staiger and Stock 1997). Always report the first-stage regression and the \(F\) statistic for the excluded instruments; a common rule of thumb flags \(F<10\) as weak.

2.8.1 Illustration: 2SLS versus efficient GMM

The simulation below has one endogenous regressor, three instruments and errors whose variance depends on the first instrument. Both estimators are consistent, but efficient GMM puts less weight on the noisy moment \(\E[z_{1i}u_i]=0\).

set.seed(2027)
one_sample <- function(n = 500) {
  Z <- cbind(1, matrix(rnorm(3 * n), n))              # constant + 3 instruments
  v <- rnorm(n)
  x <- drop(Z[, 2:4] %*% c(0.4, 0.4, 0.4)) + v
  u <- (0.5 * v + sqrt(0.75) * rnorm(n)) * exp(Z[, 2]) # endogenous, heteroskedastic
  y <- 1 + x + u
  X <- cbind(1, x)
  gmm <- function(W) solve(t(X) %*% Z %*% W %*% t(Z) %*% X, t(X) %*% Z %*% W %*% t(Z) %*% y)
  b_2sls <- gmm(solve(crossprod(Z)))                   # step 1: 2SLS
  S      <- crossprod(Z * drop(y - X %*% b_2sls)) / n
  b_gmm  <- gmm(solve(S))                              # step 2: efficient GMM
  e      <- drop(y - X %*% b_gmm)
  gbar   <- colMeans(Z * e)
  J      <- n * drop(t(gbar) %*% solve(crossprod(Z * e) / n) %*% gbar)
  c(b_2sls = b_2sls[2], b_gmm = b_gmm[2], J_reject = J > qchisq(0.95, df = 2))
}
mc <- t(replicate(1000, one_sample()))
round(c(mean_2sls = mean(mc[, 1]), mean_gmm = mean(mc[, 2]),
        sd_2sls = sd(mc[, 1]), sd_gmm = sd(mc[, 2]),
        J_rejection_rate = mean(mc[, 3])), 3)
#>        mean_2sls         mean_gmm          sd_2sls           sd_gmm 
#>            1.016            1.002            0.273            0.199 
#> J_rejection_rate 
#>            0.039

Both estimators are centred on the true slope of one. The standard deviation of efficient GMM is about a quarter smaller than that of 2SLS. The J test, with \(q-k=4-2=2\) degrees of freedom, rejects the true moment conditions at roughly the nominal 5% rate.

2.9 Two-step estimators as joint GMM

Many estimators proceed in two steps: a first step estimates a nuisance parameter \(\balpha\) (a propensity score, a first-stage regression, a selection equation), and a second step estimates the parameter of interest \(\tau\) given \(\hat\balpha\). Treating \(\hat\balpha\) as known in the second step gives wrong standard errors. Stacking both steps into one MM problem gives the correct ones automatically.

Let \(\bpsi_1(\bw_i,\balpha)\) (\(k_1\) conditions) define the first step and \(\psi_2(\bw_i,\balpha,\tau)\) the second. The stacked moment function \[ \bpsi(\bw_i,\btheta)=\begin{pmatrix}\bpsi_1(\bw_i,\balpha)\\ \psi_2(\bw_i,\balpha,\tau)\end{pmatrix},\qquad \btheta=(\balpha',\tau)', \] is just identified, and \(\bA_0\) is block lower triangular: \[ \bA_0=\begin{pmatrix}\bA_{11}&\bm 0\\ \bA_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},\quad \bA_{11}=\E\Big[\pd{\bpsi_1}{\balpha'}\Big],\ \bA_{21}=\E\Big[\pd{\psi_2}{\balpha'}\Big],\ A_{22}=\E\Big[\pd{\psi_2}{\tau}\Big]. \] Applying Theorem 2.1 and reading off the last row gives the influence representation \[ \sqrt n(\hat\tau-\tau_0)=-\frac{1}{A_{22}}\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[\psi_{2i}-\bA_{21}\bA_{11}^{-1}\bpsi_{1i}\Big]+o_p(1). \] The term \(\bA_{21}\bA_{11}^{-1}\bpsi_{1i}\) is the correction for the estimated first step. It vanishes only if \(\bA_{21}=\bm 0\), i.e. if the second-step moment does not depend on \(\balpha\) in expectation. A surprising but general feature: when the first step is efficient ML, the correction can reduce the variance, so that using an estimated nuisance parameter is better than using the true one (Hirano, Imbens, and Ridder 2003).

The leading application in this course is the inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator of an average treatment effect, where \(\balpha\) indexes a logit propensity score. Problem Set 2 derives the moment functions, and Computer Tutorial 2 estimates the model jointly, computes the stacked sandwich variance and compares it with the bootstrap.

2.10 Further applications

GMM is the natural framework whenever theory delivers orthogonality conditions rather than a full likelihood. Two examples from the slides:

  • Dynamic panel data. In \(y_{it}=\gamma y_{i,t-1}+\bx_{it}'\bbeta+\eta_i+\varepsilon_{it}\), first differencing removes \(\eta_i\) but makes \(\Delta y_{i,t-1}\) correlated with \(\Delta\varepsilon_{it}\). Lags \(y_{i,t-2},y_{i,t-3},\ldots\) are valid instruments, and their number grows with \(t\). Arellano and Bond (1991) use all of them in an efficient GMM estimator.
  • Covariate balancing propensity score. Imai and Ratkovic (2014) estimate the propensity score by GMM, combining the logit score with moment conditions that require the weighted covariate means to balance between treated and controls. The overidentified version allows a J test of the propensity score model.

2.11.1 Problem 1: The effect of smoking on birth weight (IPW as joint MM)

This problem uses the results of Problem Set 2, Problem 4.

Variable Description
bweight infant birth weight (grams)
mbsmoke = 1 if the mother smoked during pregnancy
mmarried = 1 if the mother is married
mage mother’s age (years)
medu mother’s education (years)
fbaby = 1 if first baby

We want the average treatment effect \(\tau\) of mbsmoke on bweight. The propensity score is modelled as a logit, \[ P(\texttt{mbsmoke}=1\mid X_i)=\Lambda\big(\alpha_0+\alpha_1\texttt{mmarried}+\alpha_2\texttt{mage}+\alpha_3\texttt{mage}^2+\alpha_4\texttt{fbaby}+\alpha_5\texttt{medu}\big). \]

  1. Compute the raw difference in mean birth weight between smokers and non-smokers. Why is it unlikely to be the ATE?

  2. Write the stacked moment function for \(\btheta=(\balpha',\tau)'\) from Problem Set 2 as an R function that returns the \(k+1\) sample moments, and solve the system with nleqslv. Compare \(\hat\balpha\) with glm and \(\hat\tau\) with the IPW formula evaluated at the glm propensity score. Why do they coincide?

  3. Estimate the asymptotic variance of \(\hat\btheta\) with the MM sandwich \(\hat\bA^{-1}\hat\bV\hat\bA^{-1\prime}/n\), computing \(\hat\bA\) analytically. Report the standard error of \(\hat\tau\) and compare it with the “naive” standard error that treats the propensity score as known.

  4. Compute the ATE with normalized weights (weights that sum to one within each treatment group).

  5. Estimate the standard errors of both ATE estimators by the nonparametric bootstrap (500 replications). Remember that each bootstrap replication has to re-estimate the propensity score.

2.11.2 Problem 2: Returns to schooling with college proximity (IV and GMM)

Variable Description
lwage76 log hourly wage, 1976
ed76 years of education, 1976
age76 age in 1976
black, reg76r, smsa76r = 1 if black; lives in the South; lives in an urban area (1976)
nearc4a, nearc4b = 1 if grew up near a 4-year public / private college

Following Card (1995) and B. E. Hansen (2022, Ch. 12), consider \[ \log w_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\texttt{educ}_i+\beta_2\,\texttt{exper}_i+\beta_3\,\texttt{exper}_i^2/100+\beta_4\texttt{black}_i+\beta_5\texttt{south}_i+\beta_6\texttt{urban}_i+u_i , \] with potential experience \(\texttt{exper}=\texttt{age}-\texttt{educ}-6\). Education is endogenous (ability is omitted), and so is experience, because it is a function of education. The excluded instruments are nearc4a, nearc4b, age76 and age76^2/100.

  1. Construct exper and exper2, drop observations with missing values and estimate the equation by OLS.

  2. Estimate the first stage for ed76. Test the joint significance of nearc4a and nearc4b with a heteroskedasticity-robust \(F\) test. Are the instruments strong?

  3. Count the moment conditions \(q\) and parameters \(k\). Compute 2SLS with the GMM formula and \(\bW_n=(\bZ'\bZ)^{-1}\), with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Check your results with AER::ivreg and sandwich.

  4. Compute the two-step efficient GMM estimator and its standard errors.

  5. Compute Hansen’s J statistic and its p-value. What does it test here?

  6. Compare the estimated returns to schooling across OLS, 2SLS and GMM.
